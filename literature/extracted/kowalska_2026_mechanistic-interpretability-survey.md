## Unboxing the Black Box: A Survey on Mechanistic Interpretability for Algorithmic Understanding of Neural Networks

<!-- image -->

Bianka Kowalska 1 · Halina Kwaśnicka 1

Received: 9 May 2025 / Revised: 1 July 2026 / Accepted: 16 July 2026 / Published online: 27 July 2026 © The Author(s) 2026

## Abstract

The black box nature of deep neural networks poses a significant challenge for the deploy - ment of transparent and trustworthy artificial intelligence (AI) systems. With the growing presence of AI in society, it becomes increasingly important to develop methods that can explain and interpret the decisions made by these systems. To address this, mechanistic interpretability (MI) emerged as a promising and distinctive research program within the broader field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). MI studies the inner computations of neural networks and translates them into human-understandable algorithms. It encom - passes reverse-engineering techniques aimed at uncovering the computational algorithms implemented  by  neural  networks.  In  this  paper,  we  present  a  comprehensive  survey  of mechanistic interpretability, synthesizing a rapidly growing and fragmented body of work into  a  single,  structured  reference.  We  propose  a  unified  taxonomy  of  MI  approaches and provide a detailed analysis of key techniques, illustrated with concrete examples and pseudo-code. We contextualize MI within the broader interpretability landscape, compar - ing  its  goals,  methods,  and  insights  to  other  strands  of  XAI. Additionally,  we  trace  the development of MI as a research area, highlighting its conceptual roots and the accelerat - ing pace of recent work. We argue that MI has the potential to support a more scientific understanding of machine learning systems - treating models not only as tools for solving tasks, but also as systems to be studied and understood. We intend this survey to serve as an entry point for new researchers to the field of mechanistic interpretability.

Keywords Explainable AI · Mechanistic interpretability · Survey

## 1  Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly being deployed in a wide range of tasks, from everyday applications like recommendation systems to high-risk domains such as biomet - ric recognition, autonomous vehicles, and medical diagnosis (Ali et al., 2023). The rise of transformer-based models, particularly in natural language processing (NLP), has further accelerated this adoption, enabling breakthroughs in text generation, translation, and image understanding (Lin et al., 2022). However, the size, complexity, and opacity of these models are growing just as quickly, outpacing researchers' ability to understand their internal work - ings. This combination of growing influence and persistent opacity has led to a heightened demand for AI models that are both faithful and explainable. The validation of AI-generated decisions is especially critical in high-risk areas, such as law or medicine (Barredo Arrieta et al., 2020; Srinivasu et al., 2022). Consequently, Explainable AI (XAI) emerged in response to the companies' and researchers' demands to interpret, explain, and validate neural net - works to make AI systems trustworthy.

XAI encompasses all methods and approaches to uncover the reasoning and behavior of artificial intelligence systems (Ali et al., 2023; Arsenault et al., 2025; Theissler et al., 2022). Thus, it is important to establish an understanding of common terms used in the XAI literature, despite the lack of universally accepted definitions. In general, explainability refers to understanding a system's outputs (Hamida et al., 2024), answering the why? ques - tion; whereas interpretability refers to understanding the system's internal workings (Das, 2020; Räuker et al., 2023), answering the how? question. However, this distinction is not universally agreed upon.  Lipton (2018) argues that interpretability bundles several distinct properties rather than a single, well-defined one, while  Rudin (2019) draws a sharper dis - tinction between interpretable models - which are transparent by design - and black-box models that have merely been explained after the fact, cautioning against treating the two as equivalent. Throughout this survey, we adopt the above definitions for consistency, while acknowledging this terminological plurality.

Interpretable artificial intelligence refers to AI models that are intrinsically transparent in their operation, eliminating the need for post-hoc analysis. Consequently, these models enable the identification and tracking of causal relationships (Schwalbe &amp; Finzel, 2024). Trustworthy AI is mainly associated with AI systems designed to inspire trust by priori - tizing safety, fairness, and transparency. The use of XAI is a viable approach to building trustworthy AI models (Kowald et al., 2024; Markus et al., 2021). Table 1 summarizes the key aspects of XAI.

The  growing  demand  for  transparency  in AI  systems  has  led  to  the  development  of a  vast  number of Explainable AI methods, each designed to address different aspects of interpretability across various models and applications. Researchers have proposed differ ent taxonomies to organize this diversity (Saranya &amp; Subhashini, 2023; Ali et al., 2023 ; Barredo Arrieta et al., 2020; Schwalbe &amp; Finzel, 2024). Two key distinctions recur across these taxonomies: scope (local explanations for individual predictions versus global insights into overall model behavior) and stage of application (whether explanations are integrated into the model or applied post-hoc). Within post-hoc approaches, a further distinction sepa - rates model-specific methods, tailored to a particular model, from model-agnostic methods, which operate independently of the underlying model (Speith, 2022). Other distinctions may include the format of the explanation produced, the type of input data (e.g., tabular, image, text), or the underlying problem type (e.g., classification, regression). One notable approach that emerged over the past few years is Mechanistic Interpret - ability (MI), a cognition-based set of reverse-engineering techniques aimed at uncovering the computational algorithms of neural networks (Olah, 2022; Saphra &amp; Wiegreffe, 2024). Mechanistic interpretability focuses on the internal structures of AI models - neurons, lay - ers, attention heads, and circuits - and their causal roles in computation. Unlike explainabil - ity methods, such as feature attribution (e.g., LIME Ribeiro et al. 2016 or SHAP Lundberg &amp; Lee 2017), MI seeks a deeper understanding of models, drawing inspiration from neu - roscience and systems biology. According to the proposed taxonomy, MI is typically posthoc and model-specific, similar to feature-attribution methods. However, MI differs in its primary objective: attribution methods are largely local and correlational, identifying which inputs influenced an output, whereas MI pursues global, causal accounts of the algorithm a model implements internally. This shift from "what mattered" to "how it works" is the central distinguishing contribution of MI within the broader XAI landscape. Rather than treating AI systems as a black boxes, MI researchers emphasize the importance of inner interpretability  based  on  the  premise  that  internal  components  of  neural  networks  adopt specific roles after training (Manning et al., 2020; Saphra, 2021). Identifying such com - ponents provides deeper insight into model behavior and a scientific understanding of AI. To uncover these roles, researchers employ a range of techniques rooted in pattern rec - ognition, as any non-random structure within a neural network can serve as a functional component (Kästner &amp; Crook, 2024). These techniques include methods for analyzing the representations learned by models, assessing the causal impact of specific components, and visualizing patterns within the network's structure and activations.

Mechanistic  interpretability  provides  a  systematic  characterization  of  AI  systems  as opposed  to  ad  hoc  methods  of  XAI,  which  provide  explainability  only  in  specific  con - texts  (Kästner  &amp;  Crook,  2024).  Its  primary  goal  is  to  improve AI  safety  and  build  trust by providing thorough, human-interpretable explanations of complex AI models. This is achieved by mapping low-level components, such as circuits or neurons, to elements of human-understandable algorithms and building upward to develop a holistic system-level understanding (Olah, 2022). As research in this area progresses, current efforts are increas - ingly focused on refining methodologies and scaling analyses to more complex models. The increasing interest in mechanistic interpretability is further evidenced by the growing num - ber of publications, particularly review articles.  Kästner and Crook (2024),  Grzankowski (2024), and  Rabiza (2024) situate mechanistic interpretability in a philosophical context, rather  than  surveying  its  technical  methods.    Davies  and  Khakzar  (2024)  seek  parallels between MI and cognitive science.  Bereska and Gavves (2024) present a comprehensive overview of advancements in MI, along with a taxonomy of concepts, but frame their con - tribution primarily around AI safety rather than situating MI within the wider XAI land - scape.    Rai  et  al.  (2025)  focus  on  employing  MI  in  language  models,  and    Saphra  and Wiegreffe (2024) present the divide between MI and NLP interpretability communities.  Lin et al. (2025) survey MI in multimodal models and identify open research gaps in that subdo - main.  Sharkey et al. (2025) focus on open challenges in the field and present possible future directions, without providing a taxonomy of existing techniques.

Taken together, existing surveys are either philosophically oriented, safety-focused, or scoped to a particular modality or community, and none systematically positions MI within the broader XAI field while also providing a comprehensive, technique-level taxonomy. Our survey addresses this gap: we survey the current status of mechanistic interpretability, posi - tion MI within the broader XAI community, and propose a clear definition along with a tax - onomy of techniques that spans the modalities and tasks discussed across the works above. We aim to organize the rapidly growing body of mechanistic interpretability research into a coherent conceptual and methodological framework. Despite its growing importance, the field lacks a consolidated reference that systematically covers its foundations, methods, and open challenges. This survey aims to fill this gap by providing a comprehensive overview of mechanistic interpretability. Specifically, we pursue the following objectives:

- Situating mechanistic interpretability within the broader context of mechanistic expla - nations and explainable AI (Sect. 2).
- Providing a clear definition and taxonomy of mechanistic interpretability (Sect. 2).
- Providing a comprehensive literature review on mechanistic interpretability (Sect. 3).
- Presenting the key techniques used in the field, with pseudo-code and literature exam - ples (Sect. 4).
- Outlining major challenges and opportunities in MI (Sect. 5).

The paper is structured as follows: Sect. 2 describes the philosophical background of MI and places it in the broader XAI community. It follows with a brief introduction to the core concepts  in  MI.  Section  3  presents  a  literature  review.  Section  4  describes  the  different approaches used in MI and presents notable advancements. Section 5 addresses the challenges and opportunities found in MI. Section 6 outlines future goals in MI and concludes the article.

## 2  What is Mechanistic Interpretability?

The mechanistic approach to understanding neural networks is a relatively new develop - ment within the field of AI interpretability. As this approach gains traction, it is important to situate it within the broader XAI community. In this section, we first outline the scientific and historical context of MI, followed by the presentation of a definition and taxonomy.

## 2.1  Origins of Mechanistic Interpretability

Philosophical  background. The  study  of  mechanisms  and  mechanistic  explanations is rooted in science and philosophy and has been a topic of research since the 20th cen - tury (Craver et al., 2024).  Bechtel and Abrahamsen (2005) define a mechanism as a structure performing a function in virtue of its components parts, component operations, and their organization . In other words, a mechanism is a organized structure that causes some phenomena through interactions between parts, and can be represented as a directed graph of components involved in the process. By studying the phenomenon, researchers aim to uncover the underlying mechanism and explain its functioning in terms of its causal com - ponents. This investigative process culminates in the formulation of a mechanistic explana - tion (Machamer et al., 2000). In fields like medicine (Courcelles et al., 2022; Metzcar et al., 2024), physics, and neuroscience (Bassett &amp; Sporns, 2017), mechanistic explanations play a critical role in dissecting complex systems, revealing their constituent parts, and estab - lishing causal relationships that elucidate how these components work together to produce emergent behavior (Miller, 2019).

Mechanistic  interpretability  parallels  the  way  scientists  study  physical  systems  and aligns AI research with the foundational principles of explanatory science. It conceptualizes artificial neural networks as complex systems composed of multiple mechanisms, establish - ing an analogy between AI and the human brain (Davies &amp; Khakzar, 2024; Liu et al., 2023 ; Fernando &amp; Guitchounts, 2025; Mineault et al., 2024). This approach is further supported by the previous applications of mechanistic explanations in neuroscience and cognitive sci - ences (Craver, 2007; Craver et al., 2024; Kostic &amp; Halffman, 2023; Khambhati et al., 2018). By bridging insights from neuroscience and AI, mechanistic interpretability offers a new framework for understanding artificial neural networks.

XAI background. Mechanistic interpretability first emerged in the context of interpret - ing vision models (Olah et al., 2020). With most of its early research being published on blogs and forums (see, e.g., LessWrong), the field developed separately from other inter - pretability methods. At the same time, due to the rise of advanced transformer models, the broader XAI community focused its attention on the explanations of large language models. As a result,  an  NLP  interpretability  community  emerged,  which  at  the  time  was  mostly unaware of the mechanistic research. Concurrently, many MI researchers have turned their attention to interpreting transformers (Elhage et al., 2021). Thus, two distinct communities emerged, united by the same goal: AI safety (Saphra &amp; Wiegreffe, 2024).

When MI researchers engaged with academia, a clash with the NLP interpretability com - munity emerged. Initially, many NLP researchers criticized MI research for "rediscover - ing" existing interpretability methods that had already been adopted and refined within the NLP community. This tension was fueled by differing terminologies and methodological approaches, leading to debates over the novelty and relevance of MI contributions. NLP

researchers argued that MI often overlooked the rich history and established practices of NLP interpretability, while MI proponents contended that their focus on detailed, mecha - nistic explanations provided deeper insights into model behavior. Despite these conflicts, the clash ultimately spurred a productive dialogue, encouraging both communities to reevaluate and integrate their methods, fostering a more comprehensive understanding of AI interpretability.

Although both mechanistic and NLP interpretability communities work towards similar goals,  the  term  "mechanistic"  has  not  been  widely  adopted  in  academia.  Interpretability methods may or may not be described as mechanistic, leading to a blurred line between tax - onomies and categorizations of approaches - for instance probing, which was present long before MI, has been adopted by MI researchers and is now often classified as a mechanistic method (Alain &amp; Bengio, 2017; Gurnee et al., 2023). This overlap has led to a rich, albeit complex,  landscape  of  interpretability  research,  where  methods  and  terminologies  often overlap and evolve.

## 2.2  Defining Mechanistic Interpretability

Following the discussion in the previous section, we propose a new definition for mechanis - tic interpretability: one that integrates MI with the overall inner interpretability research, but distinguishes it by focusing on the computational aspects of neural networks.

Definition. Mechanistic interpretability is the process of studying the inner computations of neural networks and translating them into human-understandable algorithms.

This definition supports the core idea of MI, which is its algorithmic approach to neural networks.  Olah (2022) compares MI to reverse-engineering a compiled computer program. Similarly to variables interacting in programs to produce a desired output, neural networks are composed of neurons, interacting through their connections via weights. Based on the premise that neurons can be understood, and therefore the connections between them, MI aims to deconstruct neural networks into interpretable subnetworks. Although a neural net - work as a whole is  a  complex  structure,  it  can  be  broken  down  into  small,  meaningful components.

The  fundamental  unit  of  neural  network  representations  is  a feature .  Features  refer to  any  interpretable  properties  or  characteristics  encoded  in  neurons.  Unlike  traditional machine  learning,  where  features  correspond  directly  to  input  data  attributes  (Bishop, 2006), MI treats them as abstractions capturing internal model properties. For instance, a feature in a vision model could be a curve detector, which responds to images containing a curve (Olah et al., 2020). Formally, they are defined as directions in the network's activation space (Elhage et al., 2022). Ideally, a single feature would correspond to a single neuron. However, in real-world neural networks, a major challenge is polysemanticity - the phe - nomenon where a single neuron encodes multiple features to accommodate the model's limited number of neurons (Olah et al., 2020; Elhage et al., 2022). A polysemantic neuron responds to different, often unrelated concepts, undermining the mechanistic approach to AI. Polysemanticity is explored in detail in Sect. 4.3.

MI seeks to interpret the connections and interactions between features by analyzing circuits -  features  linked  by  weights. Circuits are the minimal computational subgraphs of neural networks that are responsible for performing a given task. They are defined as directed, acyclic graphs. For instance, the curve detector feature is linked with downstream neurons to represent more complex features (Olah et al., 2020). Formally, in a neural net - work represented as a directed graph G = ( V, E ) , where V is the set of nodes (neurons in the network) and E is the set of weighted edges (representing weighted connections between neurons), a circuit is a subgraph within G : C = ( V C , E C ) ,  where V C ⊆ V and E C ⊆ E , that encapsulates a coherent computational process at various levels of abstraction.

Circuit discovery is the leading task defining mechanistic interpretability, and has already demonstrated promising results in both smaller and more complex models (Lieberum et al., 2023). The study of circuits could pave the way for proving the universality hypothesis - the idea that similar circuits and features are present across tasks and models. Such recurring patterns are motifs . The universality hypothesis, if true, may allow for knowledge transfer from small, toy models to state-of-the-art large-scale models (Chughtai et al., 2023; Merullo et al., 2024; Nainani et al., 2024).

Taxonomy of MI approaches. Mechanistic interpretability approaches can be organized based on different levels of analysis. Although all methods are united by the "top-down approach" aimed at unfolding input-output mechanisms responsible for a model's behav - iors,  they  employ  different  techniques,  and  the  definition  of  the  entities  analyzed  varies across studies. The proposed taxonomy divides MI approaches according to three criteria: scope, task, and nature of analysis (Fig. 2).

Scope. Scope  refers  to  the  part  of  neural  network  being  interpreted. Any  component involved in the forward pass can serve as a unit of analysis (Rai et al., 2025). The narrowest level of analysis focuses on individual neurons, identifying their role in specific tasks - for instance, identifying a neuron crucial for a given decision (Olah et al., 2020). Researchers have increasingly generalized this analysis to more complex elements, such as attention heads or whole layers (Wang et al., 2022). Broader scope, such as circuit, refers to studying structures within a network, where the connections between components play a crucial role in interpretation. At the broadest level, circuits - structures spanning multiple components - are studied as a whole. At this level, the connections between components, rather than the components themselves, play the crucial role in interpretation.

Task. The  primary objective of MI is to reverse-engineer a neural network; however, most of the current research addresses only fragments of this goal. Among these are tasks such as feature localization, circuit discovery, and feature disentanglement. Feature local - ization refers to identifying components that are responsible for specific features. Circuit discovery is the task of uncovering meaningful structures in the network. Feature disen - tanglement focuses on resolving polysemanticity. While all contribute valuable pieces to the puzzle, they fall short of providing a holistic understanding of neural networks. Researchers often combine multiple methods to obtain a more complete understanding (e.g., validating a circuit found with patching via probing or visualization (Marks &amp; Tegmark, 2024)).

Nature  of  analysis. MI  can  be  divided  into  two  main  categories:  observation-based approaches and intervention-based approaches. The key difference lies in whether the focus is  on  passively  observing  the  model's  behavior or actively modifying its components to study  the  effects.  Observation-based  approaches  concentrate  on  examining  the  internal mechanisms of a neural network without making any changes to its architecture or param - eters. These methods typically involve techniques such as visualizing weights, activations, or gradients, as well as tracing the flow of information through the network. Conversely, intervention-based approaches involve actively altering the network or its inputs to investi - gate causal relationships and gain insights into specific behaviors.

## Comparison of MI approaches.

Despite  the  rapid  growth  of  mechanistic  interpretability,  direct  comparisons  between techniques remain rare (Mueller et al., 2025a). Most studies evaluate a single method or a small set of closely related variants, on an ad-hoc task of their own choosing, making it difficult to assess whether progress reflects genuine methodological improvement or differ - ences in evaluation setup. In principle, methods as different as probing, activation patching, and sparse autoencoders could be evaluated against the same model and task using shared metrics, but such cross-method comparisons have only recently begun to emerge (Mueller et al., 2025a).

Recent benchmarking efforts have started to address this gap.  Mueller et al. (2025b) introduce the Mechanistic Interpretability Benchmark (MIB), which evaluates circuit dis - covery  and  causal  variable  localization  methods  across  multiple  tasks  and  models.  The benchmark includes well-studied tasks such as indirect object identification (Wang et al., 2022) and arithmetic (Stolfo et al., 2023). Gupta and Kwa propose InterpBench (Gupta et al., 2024), which instead evaluates methods on synthetic transformer models with known ground-truth circuits, avoiding the lack of verified circuits in naturally trained models. How - ever, to the best of our knowledge, the field still lacks interpretability benchmarks for other model families, such as CNNs.

In  this  survey,  we  do  not  conduct  an  experimental evaluation; instead, we synthesize existing findings into a structural comparison of methods' high-level properties. Table 2 compares techniques along the proposed criteria (task, scope, nature) and summarizes their main  advantages  and  disadvantages,  providing  the  kind  of  cross-method  overview  that, as  discussed  above,  remains  largely  absent  from  the  empirical  literature.  Performing  an experimental comparison at the current stage of the field would likely compare experimen - tal protocols rather than the underlying methods themselves. The absence of standardized empirical comparisons should not be interpreted as a weakness of this survey, but rather as a reflection of the current state of mechanistic interpretability.

## 3  The Rise of Mechanistic Interpretability

Methodology. Based on this and additional database analyses, we decided to focus our quantitative analy - sis on the period since 2015, reflecting the dynamic expansion of AI model interpretation methods.

The literature search was conducted in three databases: Scopus , 1 Web of Science , 2 and PubMed . 3 We encountered two major challenges when researching the mechanistic inter - pretability of neural networks:

- Exclusion : referring to papers related to mechanistic interpretability, but not mentioning their mechanistic background directly.
- Broadness : referring to papers related to mechanistic explanations in general but not to AI models.

Exclusion results from the novelty of MI in the context of AI interpretability. Many research - ers who study topics related to mechanistic interpretability may not be aware of this naming convention. Moreover, as exemplified with probing, some approaches are now considered mechanistic despite their different origins. In addition, much of the early MI research has been published in blogs and forums, thus it is not listed in research databases.

Broadness stems from the philosophical and scientific background of MI. Mechanistic explanations  are  widely  used  across  various  disciplines,  including  biology,  physics,  and engineering. This broad applicability makes it difficult to filter and curate AI-specific litera - ture, as many papers discuss mechanistic insights without being directly relevant to neural network interpretability. Consequently, the search term "mechanistic interpretability" often yields results not related to neural network interpretability.

We studied several search terms, including "mechanistic interpretability" which suffered from broadness, "mechanistic interpretability AND neural networks", which suffered from exclusion. Ultimately, we limited the scope of the search to "Mechanistic interpretability AND Machine Learning". This query both captured the literature related to interpretability of deep neural networks and excluded works related to mechanistic explanations in other disciplines.

As depicted in Fig. 3, researchers have made significant progress by expanding the XAI toolbox (Longo et al., 2024) in recent years. However, interpretable and trustworthy AI constitute only a fraction of the publications.

We observe a significant dominance of publications on Explainable AI over the others. The finding is expected, as this term has the broadest meaning and is the most popular in the research community. The narrowest term is Trustworthy , a concept that has gained popular - ity recently, and research in this area is still in its early stages. Interpretable AI often refers to self-explaining models, while there is a high demand for the interpretability of black-box models.

A similar situation is observed in the Web of Science database. ## 4.1  Feature Localization

Feature localization refers to identifying and characterizing individual neurons that encode some mportant information. In MI, the common characteristic of a neuron is its importance - a measure of their contribution to the model's prediction. Importance is usually quantified as the activation or gradient value for a given input (Dhamdhere et al., 2018). Studying indi - vidual neurons is the first step to reverse-engineer a neural network, as they can be thought of as variables in the encoded algorithm.

## 4.1.1  Probing

Probing is a technique derived from NLP to interpret and analyze the internal representa - tions learned by models (Alain &amp; Bengio, 2017). This method is based on the premise that neural networks do not acquire any new information during their forward passes but rather transform given inputs to learn increasingly meaningful representations. By training a sim - ple classifier, called a probe , on these generated representations, researchers can determine whether the network encodes any specific type of information (Belinkov, 2022). Figure 5 provides a schematic overview of probing.

Mathematically,  probing  can  be  framed  as  a  supervised  learning  problem  (Hewitt  &amp; Liang, 2019). Given a model f that generates hidden representations h i ∈ R d for each input token or sequence x i , the goal is to train a lightweight probe g that maps h i to a target property y i (e.g., part-of-speech tag, dependency label). Formally, the probe learns a function g : R d →Y , where Y is the label space, by minimizing a loss function, L ( g ( h i ) , y i ) . The performance of the probe g on a held-out test set measures how well the representations h i encode the property y i (Alain &amp; Bengio, 2017; Belinkov, 2022). In a mechanistic approach, probing is aimed at identifying whether and where the model encodes  particular  kinds  of  information,  thereby  complementing  the  mechanistic  under - standing (Bereska &amp; Gavves, 2024). Probing can identify the roles of individual neurons and the features represented in the network, aiding in the localization of information (Gurnee et al., 2023). In particular, sparse probing , which attempts to identify only a subset of neu - rons (or a single neuron) that is activated by a given input. This is achieved by limiting the classifier to k non-zero coefficients, thus creating a k-sparse probe . Sparse probing can be viewed as a neuron ranking problem - scoring individual neurons based on their importance in a given task. It filters out irrelevant neurons, highlighting the specific representations that encode linguistic or functional knowledge. However, due to the focus on sparsity, important neurons can be overlooked (Gurnee et al., 2023; Antverg &amp; Belinkov, 2022).

Gurnee et al. (2023) propose two techniques: adaptive thresholding, which constrains the value of k through iterative retraining, and optimal sparse probing which employs a cutting plane algorithm for smaller values of k .    Chowdhury et al. (2025) employ ridge regression-based probes to identify context neurons involved in the task of document rank - ing. The authors use Lasso regularization to enforce sparsity in the classifiers. These probes are applied across the layers of the RankLlama model (Ma et al., 2024), revealing multiple statistical features captured along the layers of the network.

Other examples of probing include edge probing and structural probing ,  which are complementary methods used to evaluate linguistic knowledge encoded in language model representations, differing in focus and scope. Edge probing assesses whether local pairwise relationships between words or tokens (edges) in a linguistic structure are encoded within the representations of a model. Rather than classifying individual token properties, edge probes focus on the relationships or interactions between spans, such as syntactic dependen - cies or semantic roles (Tenney et al., 2019; Choudhury &amp; Kalra, 2023).

In contrast, structural probing evaluates the encoding of global hierarchical structures, such as full syntactic dependency trees or semantic frames. Structural probes examine how well linguistic structures are embedded in model representations, capturing the relationships between all elements in a sentence. For example,  Hewitt and Liang (2019) present a struc - tural probe to identify syntax trees embedded within the representations of ELMo (Peters et al., 2018) and BERT (Devlin et al., 2019) by predicting distances or reconstructing hier - archical tree structures. Structural probing provides insight into the broader organization of linguistic information encoded in embeddings. However, the self-supervised nature of struc - tural probes raises doubts as to about whether the probe actually captures any knowledge of language models (Farquhar et al., 2023).

<!-- image -->

Taken together, probing methods offer a practical and scalable entry point into mechanis - tic analysis, requiring minimal intervention on the model. Nevertheless, their observational nature means that high probe accuracy reflects correlation rather than causation - a funda - mental limitation that motivates the interventional approaches presented in the following sections.

## 4.1.2  Lenses

In contrast to probing, lenses do not focus solely on observing a specific state of the model. Rather, they map the representations from intermediate layers to the model's vocabulary distribution, without passing them through the remaining layers of the model. By revealing how the model's predictions develop across the network, this approach makes it possible to investigate what linguistic or semantic information is encoded at different stages of the model's computation (Elhage et al., 2021; Geva et al., 2021). Note that lenses are limited to transformer architectures, as they rely on projecting residual stream activations into vocab - ulary space. Formally, a lens maps the hidden state h l at layer l to the vocabulary space by multiply - ing it with the output embedding matrix, W out , yielding the vocabulary logits, z l (Rai et al., 2025):

Overall, lens-based methods offer a complementary perspective on model representa - tions by tracing how information evolves across layers. They extend the observational logic of probing by removing the need for labeled concepts. Their main advantage lies in their ability  to  inspect  intermediate  representations  without  an  additional  correlational  model. Their limitation - that they assume linear decodability at every layer - also highlights a broader challenge in MI: the tools we use to observe a model's computations inevitably carry their own assumptions about how those computations are structured.

## 4.2  Circuit Discovery

Circuit discovery aims to identify causal graphs in neural networks - structured subnet - works of neurons and connections that encode specific computational functions. This pro - cess supports the view that neural networks implement algorithmic computations, thereby reinforcing the idea of mechanistic interpretability and moving beyond black-box expla - nations. Empirical studies have demonstrated the emergence of structured circuits across neural networks, both in small toy models (Nanda et al., 2023; Zhong et al., 2023 ; Kitouni et al., 2024) and large-scale real-world networks (Lieberum et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2022). These findings reinforce the view that neural networks, despite their complexity, operate through interpretable and decomposable computational structures rather than relying solely on distributed, uninterpretable representations.

The principal framework for circuit discovery is based on Causal Mediation Analysis (CMA). CMA is a method derived from causal inference used to study systems of depen - dencies, typically represented by directed acyclic graphs (Pearl, 1995). It builds upon the causal framework by including mediators in its analyses - intermediate elements in a sys - tem that influence the final outcome. In neural networks, causal mediation analysis can be used to investigate circuits by treating internal model components (e.g., neurons) as media - tors between inputs and outputs (Vig et al., 2020). Figure 7 illustrates a causal graph with a mediator.

The core method derived from CMA in mechanistic interpretability is patching (also known as interchange intervention Geiger et al. 2024) - a method employed to study and understand the internal computations of a machine learning model by modifying - patching - its internal components during inference. The objective is to identify specific components of the model (e.g., particular neurons, attention heads, layers) that are causally responsible for certain behaviors or outputs. Patching stands as a fundamental approach in MI, as it is by definition  not  limited  to  studying  only  the  input-output  relations  but  emphasizes  the importance of internal components (Vig et al., 2020). Patching aims to uncover computa - tional circuits by intervening in model components and observing the resulting changes in output. This technique views models as causal graphs in which internal components (such as neurons) form causal pathways connecting inputs to outputs (Stolfo et al., 2023 ; Vig et al., 2020; Geiger et al., 2021; Finlayson et al., 2021).

Activation patching is the primary technique derived from causal interference. The activa - tion-based approach focuses on modifying or replacing the activations of neurons within a neural network model. Its primary use is to investigate the role of specific neurons or layers in the model's decision-making process by observing how the changes in activations impact the model's output (Bereska &amp; Gavves, 2024). ## 4.2.2  Attribution Patching

Attribution patching emerged as a response to the computational complexity of the standard procedure of activation patching (Nanda, 2023). Instead of performing a forward pass for each substitution, this approach uses a gradient-based approximation to find a linear estimate between the corrupted and clean pass. Attribution patching allows researchers to identify the circuit in only two forward passes and one backward pass, while simultaneously reducing the need for human supervision during the process (Ferrando &amp; Voita, 2024).  Syed et al. (2023) extend this approach by proposing edge attribution patching , an automated circuit discovery algorithm that obtains the most important edges in a given task.  Hanna et al. (2024) further build upon edge attribution patching by integrating gradients to increase the faithfulness of the discovered circuits.

## 4.2.3  Path Patching

Path patching constrains the interventions to a specific path in the model, leaving the rest of  the  network  untouched.  The  goal  is  to  determine  whether  the  patched  path  causally contributes to a specific behavior or output. In contrast to other patching methods, path patching investigates the interactions of components in a network on each other (Hanna et  al.,  2023).   Wang et al.  (2022) show its application in discovering a circuit in GPT-2

small.  Goldowsky-Dill et al. (2023) generalize the idea for any number of paths, modeling the network as an arbitrary computational graph.

Activation patching and its variants (attribution, path) represent a significant step toward causal rather than merely correlational interpretability. This causal framing is what distin - guishes the patching family from probing and lens-based approaches. Nevertheless, though the idea of localizing important components in neural networks through interventions seems sensible, researchers have questioned the faithfulness of such interpretations. Even when an intervention measurably affects model response, it is not sufficient to clearly establish that the targeted component plays the specific causal role attributed to it Wang and Veitch (2024). The effect may instead reflect confounding factors, distributed redundancy, or the intervention pushing the model out of its training distribution.  Hase et al. (2023) question the reliability of mechanistic understanding in locating model behavior. Their experiments show that successfully editing a behavior at a given location does not confirm that this loca - tion is where the original computation actually occurs. Current research aims to increase both the interpretability  and  the  faithfulness  of  circuits  by  optimizing  intervention  tech - niques (Wang &amp; Veitch, 2024; Stoehr et al., 2024). Reliable validation of discovered circuits remains one of the central open challenges of the field.

## 4.2.4  Ablation Study

Ablation,  also  known  as  knockout,  is  another  technique  derived  from  causal  inference. Although both ablation studies and patching involve the manipulation of a system's compo - nents, they differ in their specific approaches and objectives. Ablation is primarily focused on  removing  or  disabling  certain  elements  within  the  system  to  observe  the  resulting changes in behavior, thus identifying which components are essential for the functionality of the system (Meyes et al., 2019). In contrast to patching, ablation does not seek to under - stand the causal relationships between components; but rather their causal importance (Lan et  al.,  2024;  Li  et  al.,  2024).  The  removal  of  components  is  usually  performed  via zero ablation ,  which sets their values to zero, and mean ablation ,  which replaces their values with the average values across a training distribution. However, zero ablation often results in noisy results, as setting components to zero may introduce artificial disruptions that do not naturally occur in the model (Wang et al., 2022). A novel approach is optimal ablation , proposed by  Li et al. (2024), that sets the value to a constant that yields the minimal loss of the ablated model.

Ablation has been applied to various components of neural networks.  Ghorbani et al. (2020) introduced Neuron Shapley, a framework employing zero ablation to identify the crucial neurons. In their work, they quantify the importance score of neurons based on their estimated Shapley value, taking interaction between components into account (e.g., if two neurons are both required to improve the performance of the model). Alternatively,  Olsson et al. (2022) study the causal importance of induction heads in in-context learning.  Geva et al. (2023) and  Fierro et al. (2025) knock out attention weights to investigate how GPT models extract factual knowledge.  Li et al. (2024) focus on disabling edges responsible for bad behaviors in models (e.g., hallucinations) using targeted edge ablation , and  GarcíaCarrasco et al. (2024) combine ablation with the logit lens to detect vulnerabilities in GPT-2 Small Radford et al. (2019).

<!-- image -->

Another example of ablation study is causal scrubbing ,  a  generalized  approach  that aims to test hypotheses about why a model behaves in a specific way. In contrast to other causal approaches, scrubbing begins with establishing a hypothesis about the circuits. The algorithm introduced by  Chan et al. (2022) is as follows:

1.  Define the components and pathways that the hypothesis claims are responsible for a mechanism.
2.  Intervene on parts of the model that are not specified by the hypothesis.
3.  Evaluate the output of the model - if the hypothesis is correct, it should remain unchanged.

In  Chan  et  al.  (2022),  the  interventions  were  applied  using resampling  ablation ,  which replaces the values of the components using any value they had on other inputs. Due to the restriction on the intervention space, causal scrubbing performs less interventions than other methods. However, it relies heavily on the hypotheses and can misidentify correlated features (Jenner et al., 2023). As such, causal scrubbing is best understood as a validation tool for hypotheses generated through less constrained methods, rather than a discovery tool in its own right.

Overall, circuit discovery represents the central methodological direction in mechanistic interpretability, shifting the focus from identifying isolated neurons toward understanding distributed  computational  pathways. While recent advances have substantially improved automation  and  scalability,  reliable  validation  of  discovered  circuits  remains  one  of  the central open challenges of the field (Hanna et al., 2024; Hase et al., 2023).

## 4.3  Feature Disentanglement

Feature disentanglement methods aim to resolve the issue of polysemanticity, which repre - sents a significant challenge to mechanistic interpretability. Ideally, a neuron would respond to a single feature, making the network monosemantic and easier to interpret. However, in practice, neurons are often observed to be polysemantic, meaning that they respond to mul - tiple, often unrelated concepts (Goh et al., 2021; Olah et al., 2017). The primary reason for polysemanticity is the substantial number of features that the network attempts to accommo - date within its limited number of neurons (Olah et al., 2020). Furthermore,  Lecomte et al. (2024) highlight the phenomenon of incidental polysemanticity , where a network, despite having enough neurons, still overlaps representations of distinct features.

The superposition hypothesis provides a theoretical framework to explain this behavior, proposing that polysemanticity results from the compression of features into a limited representational space. The features are encoded not only in the n -dimensional activation space of a network but also in the almost orthogonal directions, as illustrated in Fig. 9.  Elhage et  al.  (2022)  demonstrate  that  superposition  emerges  naturally  in  neural  networks.  Fur - ther,  Scherlis et al. (2025) show that polysemanticity depends on the allocated importance of features, i.e., important features are encoded orthogonally to other features. Other expla - nations behind polysemanticity include redundancy introduced during training (e.g., due to  random dropout), or correlations in natural data that encourage neurons to share fea - tures (Chan, 2024).

The goal of feature disentanglement is to identify all the features encoded by the network, thereby providing a clearer understanding of its internal representations. Disentanglement can be approached in two ways: ad-hoc, by designing models without superposition (Elhage et al., 2022), and post-hoc, by employing sparse coding to describe how features are repre - sented in models with superposition (Sharkley et al., 2022).

## 4.3.1  Monosemanticity in Models

The motivation  behind  designing  models  with  monosemantic  neurons  is  their  increased interpretability, as such neurons can be easily mapped to features. One possibility is the use of linear models, which do not exhibit superposition (Elhage et al., 2022). In non-linear networks, superposition can be reduced by decreasing the sparsity of features. However, this forces models to learn only the top n important features.  Jermyn et al. (2022) engineer monosemanticity in toy models and show that increasing the number of neurons per layer reduces polysemanticity, yet leads to increased computational cost. The benefits of mono - semantic models are a subject of ongoing debate within the research community;  Wang et  al.  (2024)  demonstrate  that  decreasing  monosemanticity  benefits  model  performance, whereas  Yan et al. (2024) show a positive correlation between monosemanticity and model capacity. The question whether monosemanticity should be inhibited or encouraged remains unsettled.

## 4.3.2  Sparse Autoencoders

Following the superposition hypothesis, activations can be decomposed as a linear com - bination of features (directions in the activation space) (Elhage et al., 2022; Park et al., 2024). Based on this premise, a natural solution to identifying individual features encoded by a network is the implementation of sparse coding (Olshausen &amp; Field, 1997), especially sparse autoencoders (SAEs). Also known as sparse dictionary learning, the problem can be framed mathematically as follows: given an input dataset, X = [ x 1 , ..., x i ] , x i ∈ R m , and a positive integer n , find a dictionary D ∈ R m × n , that allows for the sparsest representation R = [ r 1 , ..., r i ] , r i ∈ R n so that ∥ DR - x ∥ 2 is minimized (Tillmann, 2015).

In other words, SAEs transform an entangled space into a sparse representation, enabling independent analysis of features. Such decomposition offers a great opportunity for inter - pretability, as it allows the analysis of the network's components in isolation. SAEs have been successfully employed for the visualization of word embeddings in LLMs (Arora et al.,  2018;  Panigrahi  et  al.,  2019;  Subramanian  et  al.,  2018; Yun  et  al.,  2021),  and  more recently to extract features from LLMs (Huben et al., 2024; He et al., 2024; Deng et al., 2023). In the context of mechanistic interpretability, SAEs have been integrated as part of circuit discovery pipelines (He et al., 2024; O'neill &amp; Bui, 2024; Ge et al., 2024; Marks et al., 2025). Taken together, these findings indicate that sparse autoencoders currently repre - sent the most promising direction for disentangling polysemantic representations.

SAEs are not the only approach to feature disentanglement.  O'mahony et al. (2023) proposed  disentangling  polysemantic  neurons  into  concept  vectors,  which  are  obtained from intermediate representations of images that have the highest activation values for a neuron.  Dunefsky et al. (2024) studied transcoders , a variant of SAEs which aims to recon - struct the original layer output, and demonstrated its use in circuit discovery.

Taken  together,  sparse  autoencoders  occupy  a  unique  position  in  the  MI  landscape: unlike the methods discussed above, they operate without labeled concepts or pre-specified hypotheses, instead deriving candidate features directly from the model's own activations through unsupervised learning. This makes them particularly powerful for exploratory anal - ysis  at  scale,  though  the  absence  of  ground-truth  features  means that the interpretability of recovered directions must still be validated through human inspection or downstream causal testing. For instance,  Chanin et al. (2025) show that as SAEs scale up, hierarchically related features tend to split into finer sub-features, but this splitting is unstable. Seemingly monosemantic latents can fail to fire where expected, with their activation instead absorbed by more specific child features - a failure mode unresolved by simply adjusting SAE size or sparsity. Such failures illustrate why recovered features cannot be taken at face value without further validation.

## 5  Challenges and Opportunities

Despite its potential, mechanistic interpretability still encounters numerous challenges. In this section, we address the major issues related to MI and outline some potential opportuni - ties and applications.

## 5.1  Challenges

Mechanistic interpretability promises to fully uncover the computations of neural networks. However, many researchers have posed the question of whether achieving this ambitious goal is truly possible. Current research is largely limited to dissecting toy models or small fragments of larger neural networks. The narrow scope of MI research may lead to "cherrypicked" achievements, with methods lacking broader evaluation (Räuker et al., 2023). We outline the major challenges facing the field of mechanistic interpretability in Table 4, fol - lowed by a detailed description of the key problem areas.

Spurious  explanations  vs.  genuine  understanding. A  key  challenge  in  mechanistic interpretability is distinguishing between explanations that accurately reflect the model's internal  computations  and  those  that  are  merely  artifacts  of  human-imposed  narratives. Cherry-picked results alone are insufficient to understand neuron behavior. Currently, with no unified evaluation techniques, MI findings still rely heavily on human interpretation. This may lead to false assumptions about a model's reasoning process, undermining the reliability of MI studies (Elton, 2022).  Madsen et al. (2024) discuss a concrete instance of  this  problem  in  probing  research.  Early  BERTology  work  (Rogers  et  al.,  2020)  took high probing accuracy as evidence that neural representations encode linguistic properties directly. However,  Hewitt and Liang (2019) showed that probes trained on control tasks - where labels are assigned arbitrarily rather than reflecting any real linguistic property - can achieve comparably high accuracy. This indicates that a probe's success partly reflects its own capacity to fit arbitrary patterns, rather than demonstrating that the underlying repre - sentation genuinely encodes the claimed structure.

Sharkey et al. (2025) and  Räuker et al. (2023) emphasize the importance of validating hypotheses about models' behavior and the development of interpretability benchmarks. The comparison and validation of MI pursuits is only possible through the utilization of standardized evaluation techniques. Faithful interpretations should capture the true causal mechanisms driving model behavior rather than providing post hoc justifications that appear convincing but lack real explanatory power. Recent work by  Méloux et al. (2025) addresses the identifiability of MI explanations, assessing both the uniqueness of discovered circuits and the algorithms they are claimed to implement. Their experiments reveal non-identifi - ability across every level of analysis: multiple distinct circuits can each reproduce the same model behavior; a single circuit can be assigned multiple competing interpretations; several different algorithms can be equally well causally aligned with the network; and a single algorithm can be causally aligned with different subspaces of the network. Together, these findings challenge the assumption that models implement unique, well-defined circuits for a given behavior, underscoring the need for explicit identifiability criteria alongside any claim of having found "the" circuit responsible for a task.

<!-- image -->

This  identifiability  problem  reinforces  the  broader  need,  discussed  in  Sect.  2.2,  for standardized benchmarks (e.g., MIB Mueller et al. 2025b) and rigorous causal validation methods such as causal scrubbing (Chan et al., 2022). These tools should be employed to explicitly  test  whether  a  proposed  explanation  is  sufficient  to  reproduce  model  behavior under controlled interventions, rather than relying on a single, potentially cherry-picked, demonstration.

Practical deployment. Mechanistic interpretability has seen only a limited adoption in non-research contexts. The following factors have contributed to this narrow utilization: the gradual emergence of MI into the mainstream (Saphra &amp; Wiegreffe, 2024), MI research being limited to a family of models (Sharkey et al., 2025), and the continuous need for human input and validation (Bereska &amp; Gavves, 2024). Current applications remain either experimental  demonstrations  or  internal  auditing  tools,  for  example,  the  Golden  Bridge Claude (Templeton et al., 2024) and other activation steering examples (Stoehr et al., 2024 ; Häon et al., 2025).

Moreover, results obtained on toy models may not be directly applicable to large-scale neural networks in the real world. Despite the advancement in the automation of MI work - flows  and  the  scaling  up  of  techniques,  further  research  is  required  to  fully  explore  the potential of this approach to interpretability.

Universality. Interpretability  is  a  difficult  and  laborious  process  that  requires  careful analysis from a human specialist. Each individual case needs independent interpretation of the results. Therefore, knowledge transfer between models and architectures could greatly accelerate advances in mechanistic interpretability. If the universality hypothesis (Chughtai et al., 2023; Merullo et al., 2024) is proven to be true, similar structures could be identi - fied in multiple models. This would allow researchers to apply insights from one study to another.

Evidence  for  universality  so  far  comes  primarily  from  constrained  settings.  For instance,  Chughtai et al. (2023) study universality of circuits emerging in one layer MLP architecture trained on different subsets of a group composition task dataset. Their findings show that all models implement the same algorithm, but using different representations. On the other hand,  Merullo et al. (2024) further study the circuit discovered in Wang et al. (2022) and show that it implements simultaneously a different task: Colored Objects. Their findings suggest a different axis of universality - single circuit across tasks. However, the hypothesis has only been proven in toy models and for specific tasks. There is no guarantee that the results will be applicable to larger and more advanced architectures.  Friedman et al.  (2024)  present  the  limitations  of  simplified  model  when  met  with  out-of-distribution inputs. This suggests that even within a single model family, structural universality can - not be assumed without re-verification at each scale, let alone across architecturally dis - tinct models. Work in this field remains an open challenge in MI, and is closely tied to the practical deployment challenges discussed above: without stronger evidence of universality, insights from research on smaller or toy models cannot be safely assumed to generalize to large-scale models.

Internal understanding. Mechanistic interpretability requires researchers to navigate the intricate landscape of the internal components and representations of a model. However, the task of understanding and translating these inner workings into human-understandable processes is complicated by challenges such as superposition, semantic drift, and the effects of experimental interventions. Superposition entangles multiple features within the same neurons or activations, requiring careful disentanglement to isolate and interpret meaningful representations (Sect. 4.3). Individual neurons in small models have been shown to respond to  several  unrelated concepts simultaneously (Elhage et al., 2022; Scherlis et al., 2025), rather than encoding one clean, interpretable feature.

Semantic drift introduces additional uncertainty, as the meaning of features and concepts can shift across different layers of the network (Sect. 4.1, in particular lenses). For example, applying conditional probing (Hewitt et al., 2021), Hewitt et al. find that properties such as part-of-speech become accessible at deeper layers than prior, baseline-relative probing methods had suggested - illustrating how methodological choices in probing can them - selves shape conclusions about where in a network a given property "emerges."

Moreover, the very act of intervening (Sect. 4.2) can itself alter the system's behavior, potentially confounding the interpretation. For instance, ablating or patching an activation can push the model into an out-of-distribution state not encountered during training, pro - ducing effects that reflect the model's behavior under an unnatural perturbation rather than revealing  how  the  original,  unperturbed  computation  actually  worked  (Zhang  &amp;  Nanda, 2024).

Combined  with  cherry-picking  and  confirmation  bias,  these  complexities  lead  to  an incomplete or overly optimistic picture of a model's interpretability (Burns et al., 2023). As a result, ensuring rigor, transparency, and reproducibility is essential for avoiding biased conclusions  in  MI  research.  Recognizing  and  addressing  these  methodological  pitfalls is  critical  to  advancing a more reliable and scientific understanding of machine learning systems.

LLM focus. With the shift of mechanistic interpretability to transformers and language models (Saphra &amp; Wiegreffe, 2024), relatively few researchers continue to dissect and inter - pret other model families. Transformer models are particularly attractive to MI researchers, as their residual stream allows for straightforward activation steering and embedding pro - jections at any given stage. However, other model architectures remain widely deployed in real-world scenarios and require the same level of research attention.

For example,  Hamblin et al. (2023) introduce circuit pruning, a saliency-based method that decomposes a CNN into sparse subnetworks, each responsible for computing a targeted latent  feature  -  isolating,  in  some  cases,  sparser  "subcircuits"  tied  to  a  feature's  activa - tion on particular inputs. This work directly demonstrates the viability of circuit discovery in CNNs, identifying feature-specific computational substructure analogous to the circuits sought in transformer-based MI research.  Beaglehole et al. (2023) provide a rigorous math - ematical background of feature learning, offering a theoretical grounding for how features emerge during training that could inform future CNN-specific circuit analysis - a level of theoretical rigor most empirical MI work currently lacks.

CNN-specific challenges - polysemantic channels, spatially distributed representations, and the absence of attention-like routing mechanisms - are left comparatively understudied. To the best of our knowledge, there is no standardized benchmark for circuit study in CNNs, echoing the broader benchmarking gap noted in the Evaluation problems challenge above.

## 5.2  Opportunities

Despite the multiple challenges present in MI, it is still a promising area of research. The possibilities in the context of AI safety convince researchers to continue work in this field. We outline the key opportunities, followed by a detailed description.

Potential applications and benefits of applying MI are:

- Debugging : MI enables the identification of faulty reasoning or unexpected model be - havior by tracing decisions to specific circuits or internal mechanisms, helping to fix or retrain models more effectively (Olah et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022).
- Data privacy :  By understanding how models store and access information internally, MI can help reveal whether sensitive training data is memorized or indirectly encoded, aiding privacy audits (Carlini et al., 2023).
- Robustness : MI can highlight the features and mechanisms that models rely on, expos - ing vulnerabilities to adversarial inputs or distribution shifts and guiding strategies to make models more robust (Burns et al., 2023; Ilyas et al., 2019).
- Control :  Gaining  mechanistic  insight  opens  the  possibility  of  directly  editing  model behavior by modifying or steering internal components, supporting efforts in fine-tuning and alignment (Sharkey et al., 2025; Meng et al., 2023).
- Human-in-the-loop : MI supports collaborative AI development by allowing humans to interpret and interact with models' decision processes, enabling feedback loops for su - pervision and correction (Chung, 2021).
- Trustworthiness : By revealing the internal logic behind model outputs, MI can provide evidence of consistent, rational behavior, supporting confidence in AI systems used in high-stakes domains.

AI safety. Mechanistic interpretability is focused on establishing the roles of components in neural networks - neurons, attention heads, or whole layers. MI not only relies on the input-output relations, but also studies the causal dependencies in between, thus dissecting the model's decision-making process step by step. This makes MI methods reliable tools for locating features - both valuable, such as factual information (Meng et al., 2022; Geva et al., 2023; Fierro et al., 2025) or truth representations (Marks et al., 2024), and dangerous, such as hallucinations (García-Carrasco et al., 2024; Li et al., 2024; Yu et al., 2024) or toxic neurons (Yang et al., 2024).  Li et al. (2025) further extend this idea by locating and remov - ing redundant parameters, demonstrating that MI insights can be applied to design more robust and efficient models.

Mechanistic interpretability seeks to elucidate the inner workings of neural networks in order to increase their transparency and trustworthiness. MI has been applied in the task of knowledge localization, debugging, and removal of harmful components.

Algorithmic understanding of neural networks. Mechanistic interpretability aims to identify how information is processed within neural networks. This involves reverse-engi - neering internal circuits and structures, and expressing them in human-interpretable terms. MI offers a more granular understanding of a model's decision-making processes by recov - ering computational routines that resemble hand-engineered algorithms. Achieving algo - rithmic understanding holds significant promise for improving our ability to reason about model generalization, identify failure modes, and precisely intervene on internal behavior.

For instance,  Chen et al. (2024) identified a circuit responsible for computing document relevance in the context of document ranking.

Real-world applications. While much of the foundational work in MI has focused on general-purpose AI systems, recent studies have explored its application in specific domains. We highlight finance and medicine as illustrative domains, as both are high-stakes, heavily regulated fields where the need for transparent, auditable AI decision-making is particularly acute, making them natural test cases for the practical applicability of MI - and, in the case of medicine, for illustrating the current gap between this need and existing work. For instance, in finance,  Golgoon et al. (2024) applied mechanistic interpretability techniques to GPT-2 Small, aiming to uncover how the model identifies potential violations of Fair Lending laws. To the best of our knowledge, no comparable application of mechanistic interpretability has yet been reported in medicine. Nevertheless, prior work has emphasized the necessity of transparent and interpretable AI systems in clinical settings (Band et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024), suggesting that MI - with its emphasis on causal, mechanism-level explanations rather than correlational ones - could be particularly well-suited to domains where under - standing why a model reached a decision is as important as the decision itself.

## 6  Conclusions

Mechanistic interpretability has emerged as a promising research program within the broader landscape of explainable artificial intelligence. In recent years, deep learning has achieved substantial progress across diverse domains, including natural language processing, com - puter  vision,  and  scientific  discovery.  Despite  these  advances,  a  fundamental  challenge remains: understanding the mechanisms by which these models generate their predictions. MI addresses this issue by aiming to uncover the underlying computations within neural networks, providing insight into the specific mechanisms that give rise to observed behav - iors. This survey sought to provide a comprehensive entry point into the field of MI. We introduced MI by providing its historical and scientific context (Sect. 2). We presented a unified taxonomy of MI approaches (Sect. 2) and explored in detail multiple techniques, provided examples and pseudo-code for representative MI methods (Sect. 4). Our literature review demonstrated the rapidly growing interest in the field (Sect. 3).

One of the central promises of mechanistic interpretability lies in its potential to move beyond  post-hoc  correlations  and  surface-level  attributions  -  common  in  other  XAI approaches - toward causal, structural, and component-level understanding of neural net - work behavior. MI aspires to uncover algorithmic structures that are implemented within the weights and activations of trained models. By reverse-engineering these internal mecha - nisms, researchers can gain insight into the representations learned by the model, the deci - sion-making procedures it implements, and the ways in which it generalizes (or fails to generalize) across tasks and inputs.

A strong mechanistic understanding is advantageous in a number of ways. MI could improve model debugging and failure mode analysis by identifying the specific pathways that contribute to undesirable behaviors. It may enable model editing - the ability to inter - vene on internal components to change a model's outputs in a controlled and interpretable way. An important direction for future research is the transfer of circuits between models, addressing whether and how discovered functional circuits from one model can be used in another. An additional area worth exploring is the representation and integration of different modalities in mechanistic circuits. Furthermore, MI holds the promise of supporting safety and alignment efforts,  particularly  in  the  context  of  powerful  foundation  models,  where understanding the internal dynamics of goal representation, deception, or power-seeking behavior may be critical. MI could also contribute to the development of more scientifically grounded understanding of AI, with machine learning systems serving not only as effective tools but also as objects of study in their own right - similar to the manner in which the study of natural intelligence is conducted in neuroscience and cognitive science.

<!-- image -->

At the same time, the field of MI is still in its early stages and many challenges remain. Among these are scaling interpretability techniques to the complexity of frontier models, developing principled evaluation frameworks for mechanistic claims, and determining the limits of human comprehensibility when confronted with distributed representations and high-dimensional feature spaces. Open questions also remain regarding the degree to which mechanistic insights transfer across architectures, training regimes, or tasks.

We view this article as a foundational step toward consolidating the field of mechanis - tic  interpretability.  By  providing  a  coherent  definition,  a  taxonomy  of  techniques,  and  a structured overview of the current research landscape, we hope to support further theoreti - cal development, methodological refinement, and interdisciplinary dialogue. The long-term vision of MI is ambitious: to build a science of deep learning systems that enables not only prediction and control but also understanding.
